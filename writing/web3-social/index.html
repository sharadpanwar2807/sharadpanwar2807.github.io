Can Web3 social break Big Tech's grip?

Lens, Farcaster and DeSo promise social media where you own your identity and audience. The idea is strong. The hard part is giving people a reason to move.

Web3Platforms

First published on ByteMeData in January 2024. Revised in October 2026. The projects and features described reflect the landscape at the time of writing.

Social media started small. SixDegrees launched in 1997, and the platforms that followed now take hours of most people's days. The arrangement has a familiar shape: users create the content, and a handful of companies own the audience, the data and the revenue.

Web3 social is an attempt to change who owns what.

The core idea: a portable social graph

Your social graph is the record of who you follow and who follows you. Today it is locked inside each platform. If you leave, you start from zero.

Web3 social protocols store that graph on open infrastructure instead. Any app can read it, so your identity, followers and posts can move with you between apps. If an app treats you badly, you leave and keep your audience.

Vitalik Buterin and co-authors pushed the idea further with soulbound tokens: non-transferable tokens that record a person's affiliations and credentials, as building blocks for what they called a decentralised society.

The main projects (as of early 2024)

Lens Protocol

Built on Polygon, Lens represents profiles and posts as tokens, creating a social graph that users own. Apps built on it included Hey, a Twitter-style client, Phaver, a mobile app that eased users in with familiar logins, and Orb, aimed at professional networking.

Farcaster

Founded by former Coinbase executives, Farcaster runs an open social network that anyone can build a client for, with Warpcast as the best-known app.

DeSo

DeSo took a different route: a dedicated layer-1 blockchain built only for social applications, designed to handle social media's volume of data and to let creators monetise directly.

What stands in the way

User numbers are still a small fraction of mainstream platforms, and the reasons are practical.

  • Experience. Wallets, keys and fees are friction mainstream users won't tolerate. The apps that grew hid them.
  • Scale. Social networks generate huge volumes of data. Storing it on-chain is expensive, so most protocols keep some of it off-chain, which reintroduces trust.
  • Moderation. Open networks still need to deal with spam, scams and abuse. Who decides, and how, is unresolved.
  • A reason to switch. Privacy and ownership matter to some people. Most people move for friends and content, not principles.

The real test

Web3 social will not win by being Twitter with a token. It needs something Web2 platforms cannot copy: audiences creators truly own, new ways to earn from them, and communities that can't be switched off by one company's decision. Until then, it remains a promising idea with a distribution problem.
