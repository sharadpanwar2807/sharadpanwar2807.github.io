DePIN, explained: paying the crowd to build infrastructure

Decentralised physical infrastructure networks use tokens to get ordinary people to supply GPUs, hotspots and storage. How the model works, and where it can break.

Web3Infrastructure

First published on ByteMeData in January 2024 as "DePIN: Next Big Thing in Crypto?". Revised in October 2026, with a section on the risks added. Figures are from Messari's State of DePIN 2023 report as cited at the time.

Most crypto projects live entirely on-chain. DePIN, short for decentralised physical infrastructure networks, is different. It uses crypto incentives to get ordinary people and companies to supply real-world hardware: wireless hotspots, storage, GPUs, sensors and more.

How it works

The model has three parts.

  1. Supply. Anyone with the right hardware connects it to the network: a hotspot on a roof, spare disk space, an idle GPU.
  2. Rewards. The network pays those providers in its token, usually based on how much useful capacity they supply.
  3. Demand. Customers pay to use the combined capacity, often more cheaply than from a single large provider.

Early on, token rewards cover the gap while there are few paying customers. The hope is that demand grows until real usage, not new tokens, pays the providers.

Why it got attention

In its State of DePIN 2023 report, the research firm Messari argued that DePIN could add around $1 trillion to global GDP over the next decade, and counted more than 650 tokens across compute, wireless, storage and AI services. Well-known examples include Filecoin for storage, Helium for wireless and Render for GPU computing. I later worked at Aethir, a decentralised GPU cloud network built on the same idea.

The appeal is that DePIN ties crypto to something physical and useful, rather than to speculation alone.

Where it can break

Looking at DePIN now through a risk lens, the model has clear weak points.

  • Subsidised demand. If token rewards are the main reason providers join, the network can look busy while few customers actually pay. When the token price falls, providers leave.
  • Quality control. Anonymous providers vary in reliability. Enterprise customers need guarantees a crowd can struggle to give.
  • Fake supply. Wherever rewards depend on reported capacity or location, some providers will try to fake it. Verification is a security problem, not just an engineering one.
  • Regulation. Physical hardware sits in real jurisdictions, under telecoms, data and tax rules.

The question to ask any DePIN project

How much of the money reaching providers comes from paying customers, and how much from newly issued tokens? A network where the first number keeps growing is building infrastructure. A network that depends on the second is running on its own token price.
