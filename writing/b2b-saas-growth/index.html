Six B2B SaaS companies and the one growth move each got right

Zendesk, Calendly, Dropbox, HubSpot, Xero and Airtable all grew in crowded markets. Each leaned hard on one mechanism. Here is what it was, and when it works.

GrowthB2B SaaS

First published on ByteMeData in December 2022, when I was a growth marketer at Adaface. Revised in October 2026. Company figures are as reported at the time of writing and have not been updated.

Almost any B2B SaaS idea already has a competitor. Usually several. The companies that break out of a crowded category rarely win on features alone. They win because one growth mechanism is built into how the product is sold, used or shared, and they push it harder than anyone else.

Before any of that works, though, there is a step most teams rush.

Start with positioning

Positioning is the answer to a buyer's real question: what does this do for me, compared with what I use now? Buyers never judge a product on its own. They judge it against the alternatives in their head, including doing nothing.

Getting it right means having clear answers to four things:

  • The pain. Which specific problem does the product remove, and for whom?
  • The buyer. Who signs off, who uses it day to day, and what each of them cares about.
  • The alternatives. How buyers see you next to competitors, and next to spreadsheets and habit.
  • The one difference. A single point that makes you distinct and still matters to the buyer. One, not five.

With that settled, here are six companies and the move that carried each of them.

1. Zendesk: make the buying decision easy

Customer service software has a reputation for steep learning curves. Zendesk's bet was a product people could use without training. By 2022 it reported around $1.3 billion in annual recurring revenue from about 150,000 paying customers.

One of its quieter wins was the pricing page. Earlier versions made buyers click into a comparison table to find out which plan included which feature. Zendesk cut this back to three core plans plus enterprise, with features laid out side by side. Late in the buying journey, a buyer comparing plans is close to paying. Any confusion at that moment loses deals that marketing has already paid to win.

Takeaway
  • Treat the pricing page as a conversion tool, and test it like one.
  • Fewer plans, clearly compared, beat more plans hidden behind clicks.

2. Calendly: let every user recruit the next one

Every time someone sends a Calendly link, the person receiving it sees the product working. If booking a meeting feels effortless, some of them start sending their own links. That loop took Calendly to around 10 million users, with reported recurring revenue above $85 million by 2022.

A truly viral product has intrinsic virality, meaning that it's designed around something that naturally involves other people.Oji Udezue, then VP of Product at Calendly

The key word is intrinsic. Referral bonuses bolted onto a single-player product rarely create this effect. The sharing has to be the point of using it.

Takeaway
  • Look for the moment your product naturally touches a non-user, and make that moment excellent.
  • If you add incentives, make them two-sided so both people gain.

3. Dropbox: give away the first taste

Dropbox gives enough free storage for documents, then charges once you need more. It reported around 700 million registered users and $1.9 billion in revenue in 2020.

Freemium does two jobs at once. It is the acquisition channel, and it is onboarding: by the time someone upgrades, they already know the product, so Dropbox spends little teaching paying customers. Users who never pay still bring others in.

It only works under two conditions. The product must deliver value within minutes, and the free tier must hit a natural limit that serious users grow into.

Takeaway
  • Need more paying users fast? A free trial. Need reach and word of mouth? Freemium.
  • Design the free limit around the moment a user is already hooked.

4. HubSpot: teach the market

HubSpot did not just sell marketing software. It popularised the idea of inbound marketing, then became the default place to learn it. By 2021 it was reported at $1 billion in ARR, with a large share of customers arriving through word of mouth, search and its blog.

The engine is useful content written for one clearly defined buyer: research, guides, templates and opinions on where sales and marketing are going. Each piece answers a real question and leaves the reader slightly better at their job. Search traffic and backlinks follow.

Takeaway
  • Pick one buyer and write for them. Chasing every persona dilutes the whole engine.
  • Keep helping customers after they pay. Retention content is still content marketing.

5. Xero: sell through the person the buyer trusts

Small business owners rarely choose accounting software alone. Their accountant usually decides. Xero built a partner programme for accountants and bookkeepers: use Xero for your clients, and get leads, offers and status in return. Xero's 2016 investor briefing said partners drove more than 90% of paid subscriptions in its most developed markets, Australia and New Zealand.

The same partners became a source of relevant backlinks, which lifted Xero in search. One relationship produced two channels.

Takeaway
  • Find who your buyer asks before buying, and make that person your channel.
  • Relevant links from your own industry outweigh many generic ones.

6. Airtable: make something hard feel easy

Relational databases are powerful and intimidating. Airtable put them behind the familiar face of a spreadsheet, so people with no technical background could build real tools. In 2021 it raised money at a valuation of $5.77 billion.

Templates and search helped, but the core growth driver was the product: a simpler way to do something people already wanted to do.

Takeaway
  • Look for a valuable tool people avoid because it is hard. Make it easy.

The pattern

None of these companies did everything. Each found the place where its product and its buyer naturally met, whether that was the pricing page, the shared link, the free tier, the blog, the accountant or the interface, and invested there far more than competitors did. Clear positioning comes first. Then pick one mechanism and make it unfairly good.
